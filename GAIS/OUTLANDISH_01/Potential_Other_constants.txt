### Physical Measurements vs. Mathematical Constants of Nature
Constants with physical units—like the speed of light ($c$), Planck’s constant ($h$), Newton's gravitational constant ($G$), or the electron charge ($e$)—**cannot** be calculated to $10^{12}$ digits. They must be measured in laboratories using physical detectors, which max out at **10 to 14 decimal places** of precision due to quantum sensor limits.

However, modern theoretical physics (quantum field theory, general relativity, statistical thermodynamics, and chaos) is fundamentally governed by **dimensionless mathematical constants**. These can be derived from pure theory, and because fast-converging series exist for them, they **can be calculated to $10^{12}$ (1 trillion) digits and beyond**.

In fact, **`y-cruncher` was created specifically for this purpose.** (The "y" in *y-cruncher* actually stands for the Greek letter $\gamma$, the Euler-Mascheroni constant)!

### What About Chaos Theory Constants (e.g., Feigenbaum's $\delta$)?
You might wonder about the **Feigenbaum constant** ($\delta \approx 4.66920...$), which governs the universal rate at which turbulent fluids and chaotic systems transition into chaos. 

While it is one of the most important constants in nature, **it cannot be calculated to $10^{12}$ digits**. 
* Unlike $\pi$, $e$, and $\zeta(3)$, which have hypergeometric series that converge exponentially via binary splitting ($O(N \log^2 N)$), Feigenbaum's $\delta$ requires root-finding across infinite-dimensional functional spaces. 
* The current world record for $\delta$ is only a few tens of thousands of digits—nowhere near 1 trillion.
